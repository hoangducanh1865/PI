\section{Improvement: a reference-free 3-D action-consistency score}
\label{sec:improve}

\paragraph{Idea.}
Every score in Tab.~\ref{tab:metrics} except the VLM judges needs the real video of the same actions, which does not exist when a world model is used as a simulator.
What always exists is the commanded action sequence.
We therefore read the actions back from the generated video with an inverse dynamics model (IDM) and compare them with the commanded ones.
We use NVIDIA Cosmos3-Nano \cite{nvidia2026cosmos3} in its inverse-dynamics mode for Bridge (domain \texttt{bridge\_orig\_lerobot}), with no training of ours.

\paragraph{Decoding the IDM output.}
The model returns 10 numbers per step.
Following NVIDIA's own encoder for this domain \cite{nvidia2026framework}, they are the step's relative pose $T_i^{-1}T_{i+1}$ (translation and the first two columns of the rotation matrix) and the commanded gripper, quantile-normalised as $\hat a = 2(a-q_{01})/(q_{99}-q_{01})-1$ with the published statistics, in a fixed frame derived from the Bridge state.
We invert these steps exactly and integrate them from the true first pose; nothing is fitted.
One convention differs between data sources: the Bridge states shipped with IRASim store the roll of the LeRobot version used by NVIDIA plus $\pi$ (median 2.47\,rad in our clips against 0.00 in LeRobot), so $R_{\text{LeRobot}}=R_{\text{IRASim}}R_x(\pi)$.
Encoding our states with NVIDIA's code and decoding them with ours returns them within $2\cdot10^{-4}$\,mm.
The score of a clip is the mean distance between the recovered and the commanded end-effector positions over frames 1 to 15.

\paragraph{Validation on real clips (105).}
Recovered relative motion correlates with the recorded one ($r=0.92$, $0.93$, $0.88$ for $x,y,z$; $0.81$ to $0.92$ for the rotation entries); the mean path error is 1.50\,cm (95\% bootstrap interval 1.36 to 1.68) for clips that move 25\,cm.
The same model on the still video scores 9.06\,cm, as bad as assuming no motion (9.32): it reads the motion from the frames, not from a prior.
The gripper command is recovered with balanced accuracy 0.87 (0.50 for always predicting ``open'').

\paragraph{Generated videos.}
Read through the same IDM, Frame-Ada scores 1.81 and 1.84\,cm (two seeds): worse than the real videos ($p<10^{-5}$, paired) but on 27 to 29\% of clips better, because the IDM's own error on a clip is shared by the real and the generated video (Spearman 0.81 between the two).
Its per-clip score correlates with LPIPS ($\rho=0.52$), but the part that exceeds the real video's score barely does ($\rho=0.21$; 2-D NDTW $0.09$): the 3-D score measures something the pixel and image-track scores do not.
Known corruptions (Tab.~\ref{tab:perturb}, last column) raise the score monotonically: 1.93\,cm for the original actions, 2.67 and 5.62 for random walks of 0.5 and 1.5\,cm per frame, 9.73 for zero actions (the still video: 9.52) and 15.32 for mirrored motion.
Across models (24 clips) it gives Frame-Ada 1.86 to 2.12\,cm over six seeds, Video-Ada 2.09 and 2.13, LVDM 2.15 and 2.53, VDM 10.68, the same order as the reference-based scores.

\paragraph{Use 1: best-of-N without a reference.}
For each of the 24 clips we generate six Frame-Ada videos and keep the one with the lowest 3-D score.
On the independent, reference-based scores the selection does not help measurably: PSNR 24.02 vs 23.93 for the average seed ($p=0.64$), 2-D NDTW 0.717 vs 0.709 ($p=0.37$), while choosing by PSNR itself (an oracle that needs the real video) would reach 24.86\,dB.
Seeds of Frame-Ada differ little (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}), so there is little to select from; the selector would matter for models whose samples differ more, which we leave open.

\paragraph{How to test it further.}
A score is useful if it falls when the video is wrong and only then: we test (i) its floor on real videos, (ii) its response to known corruptions, (iii) its agreement with human-like judges, and (iv) whether selecting by it improves reference-based scores.
