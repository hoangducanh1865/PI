\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A video world model for robot manipulation takes the first camera frame $I_0$ and a sequence of actions $a_{1:T}$ and predicts the video $\hat V$ the robot would record.
If the prediction is right, the model can replace a physics simulator for policy evaluation, planning, and data generation~\cite{yang2023unisim,zhu2024irasim,nvidia2025cosmos}.
Such models are compared mostly with frame-level appearance metrics against the recorded video, namely PSNR, SSIM~\cite{wang2004ssim}, and LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018lpips}, or with FVD~\cite{unterthiner2018fvd} over a set of videos.
These metrics leave two gaps.
First, they need the real video of the same actions, which does not exist when the model generates the outcome of a new action sequence, the very use of a simulator.
Second, they score how the frames look, not whether the robot in the video performs the commanded motion, whether a real arm could perform it, or whether objects move only when touched.

We reproduce IRASim~\cite{zhu2024irasim} on Bridge~\cite{walke2023bridgedata} and generate our 105 Bridge clips again from deliberately corrupted actions.
SSIM rates a still video above every corrupted generation and as high as the generation from the correct actions, and PSNR and LPIPS prefer it to the three strongest corruptions, whereas the end-effector track in the image, compared with the track in the real video, degrades steadily as the corruption grows.
Comparing tracks, however, needs the real video, which a simulator does not have for a new action sequence.
What it does have is the commanded actions, so a generated video can be checked against them instead of against a real video, which is the idea of our metric.

Inverse dynamics models, which predict the action between two frames, have been used to read actions from generated videos and execute them in a simulator or on a robot, scoring the video by task success~\cite{qin2024worldsimbench,fan2026wowwoval,zhang2026worldinworld}; such scores need a matched simulator or robot and mix the errors of the reader with those of the world model~\cite{liu2026kinebench}.
We instead compare the trajectory read from the video directly with the commanded one, and measure the reader's own error on the real videos of the same actions.

Our main contributions are: (1) an evaluation framework that lists, for each metric group, what it measures and what it cannot measure, with a perturbation test that checks whether a metric notices wrong actions, and (2) \ours{}, a reference-free metric that reads the end-effector trajectory back from a generated video with an off-the-shelf inverse dynamics model, decoded with its authors' own formulas, measures its deviation from the commanded trajectory, and checks whether the WidowX 250 arm could execute it.
Applied to IRASim, its baselines, and Cosmos-Predict2.5, \ours{} shows that the action input, not the smoothness of the frames, decides whether the robot follows the commands, and that the remaining failures concern objects rather than infeasible arm motion.
