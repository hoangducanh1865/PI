\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A video world model for robot manipulation takes the first camera frame $I_0$ and a sequence of actions $a_{1:T}$ and predicts the video $\hat V$ the robot would record.
If the prediction is right, the model can replace a physics simulator for policy evaluation, planning, and data generation~\cite{yang2023unisim,zhu2024irasim,nvidia2025cosmos}.
Such models are compared mostly with frame-level appearance metrics against the recorded video, namely PSNR, SSIM~\cite{wang2004ssim}, and LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018lpips}, or with FVD~\cite{unterthiner2018fvd} over a set of videos.
These metrics leave two gaps.
First, they need the real video of the same actions, which does not exist when the model generates the outcome of a new action sequence, the very use of a simulator.
Second, they score how the frames look, not whether the robot in the video performs the commanded motion, whether a real arm could perform it, or whether objects move only when touched.

We reproduce IRASim~\cite{zhu2024irasim} on Bridge~\cite{walke2023bridgedata} and generate the same 24 clips from deliberately corrupted actions: SSIM rates a static copy of the first frame above every corrupted video and even above the video from the correct actions, and PSNR and LPIPS rate it above the three strongest corruptions, whereas the end-effector track in the image, compared with the track in the real video, degrades steadily as the corruption grows.
Comparing tracks, however, needs the real video, which a simulator does not have for a new action sequence.
What it does have is the commanded actions, so a generated video can be checked against them instead of against a real video, which is the idea of our metric.
